# Monster Daycare

*A game by Wren.*

You run a daycare for baby monsters. Monster parents drop their little ones off in the
morning and collect them at home time. In between, it is you, a room full of tiny
monsters, and whatever they each decide they need.

Most of them are ordinary baby monsters. Some of them are **anomalies**.

## The feeling we are going for

Busy, funny, and then suddenly frightening. Ninety percent of the game is sweet chaos —
a baby wants a bottle, another one is crying, somebody wants a teddy. Then one of the
babies with a wrong shadow starts getting hungry, a countdown appears, and the whole room
changes mood.

That switch is the game. Everything below exists to make that switch land.

## The loop

1. **Drop-off.** A monster parent comes through the door and leaves a baby. The parents
   are much bigger than the children. You get a look at the baby as it arrives — and that
   look is where you decide *is this one an anomaly?*
2. **Needs.** Every baby wants things, over and over, all day. A thought bubble over its
   head shows what it wants right now, and a baby that wants something stops wandering and
   turns to look at you.
3. **Click and choose.** Click a monster and a little menu opens: pick it up, hold its
   hand, give it a cuddle, or hand over whatever you are carrying. Choose one and you walk
   over and do it.
4. **Home time.** The parents come back, one by one, and each one says whether
   they are pleased. A happy child gets you a thank-you and fifteen coins; a
   miserable one gets you told off.
5. **Next day.** More babies. More anomalies.

## How you actually play it

Clicking is the whole interface. You click a monster or a piece of furniture, a menu opens
next to it, and picking something from that menu sends you walking over to do it. You can
also walk yourself with the keys, and walking cancels whatever the menu set going.

**The menu on a baby:**

- Take their drawing (only once they have finished one)
- Give it *whatever you are carrying* (only when your hands are full)
- Give it a cuddle
- Hold its hand (it follows you around until you let go)
- Pick it up / Put it down

**The menu on the furniture:** the fridge has five foods and two drinks, the toy box has
three toys, the nap corner has blankets, and the colouring table has the crayons. You
carry one thing at a time.

## What baby monsters need

| Need | What they want | Where you get it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 🍽️ Hungry | food | the fridge |
| 🍼 Thirsty | a bottle | the fridge |
| 😴 Sleepy | a blanket | the nap corner |
| 🧸 Bored | a toy | the toy box |
| 😢 Sad | a cuddle — no object, just get there | you |
| 🖍️ Wants to draw | the crayons | the colouring table |

**They ask for one particular thing.** A hungry baby does not want "food", it wants
*crisps*, and the bubble over its head is a picture of crisps while the list down the side
says so in words. Bring it anything else and it gets *crosser*, not happier. You are never
guessing.

**Fussy eaters.** Nibbles only ever asks for socks. Pudding only ever asks for sweet
things. This works the same way ingredients work in Wren's Kitchen: every item carries
little labels, and a fussy baby only picks from the ones wearing its label. A new food we
invent works with every monster for free.

Fussiness is about food only. A baby that could never be given a drink it liked would just
be a dead end.

**Drawing.** Give the crayons to a baby that wants to draw and it settles down and
colours for a while. Then it has a picture, and a new option appears on its menu: **take
their drawing**. It is worth coins, and they love you for it.

## Anomalies

This is the heart of it. There are two kinds, and they are different in what they look
like *and* in what happens if you are late.

**They look wrong, and it is easy to see.** No guessing games, no squinting. You can tell
from across the room.

### The shadow one

An ordinary little blue monster. Perfectly normal. Except its **shadow is a horned
triangle**, and everyone else's shadow is a round blob. The body and the shadow do not
match, and that is the whole tell.

If you are late: **it breaks the door down and eats the other children.** The run is over.

### The one with the teeth

Looks like a sweet square monster with antennae, until you notice the **teeth sticking
out** — a mouth far too full of them, and two long fangs at the corners.

If you are late: it breaks the door down, but it does *not* eat anybody. It **floats up
into the air and performs a ritual**, turning into the spiky triangle from the drawing,
rising higher and spinning as it goes.

**And the ritual can be stopped.** What it actually wants is its nappy changing. A second
countdown starts — twenty-five seconds — and the only thing on its menu is *change its
nappy*. Do it and the ritual stops, it comes back down, it is perfectly happy, and you get
sixty coins. Miss that one too and the run is over.

So the toothy one is a second chance and the shadow one is not, which is the right way
round: the shadow one is the real threat.

### The one minute rule

When an anomaly has a need, a countdown starts. Sixty seconds. The game makes absolutely
sure you know:

- a big red timer at the top of the screen
- the room darkens, more and more
- a red glow under the anomaly, pulsing faster as it runs down
- the door it is going to break starts rattling

Get there in time and it is fine, it is delighted, and it pays **three times** what an
ordinary baby pays. Anomalies are worth the fear.

**Why this mechanic is good.** Everything else in the game is forgiving — a bored baby is
just a bit grumpy. The anomaly timer is the only thing that can end you, so your attention
goes to it completely, and you feel it being pulled away from everything else. A game
where everything is equally dangerous is just noise. One thing that really matters, with a
warning before it does, is much scarier.

**It is always survivable.** You always get the look at drop-off, and you always get the
full minute with warnings. Losing should feel like *I was too slow*, never *that was
unfair*.

## Who you are

You pick a skin on the front screen, both from Wren's character sheet:

- **The Teacher** — long hair, glasses, a heart on her top
- **The Robot** — a slab head on a springy segmented neck

You also name your own daycare, and the name you type sits in the corner all game.

There is music — a little synthesised loop with no sound files behind it — plus a chime
when you get something right, a buzz when you do not, and a heartbeat that gets faster as
an anomaly's fuse burns down. The button at the top turns it all off.

## How a day goes

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Day 1 | two babies, no anomalies. Learn where everything is. |
| Day 2 | three babies, no anomalies. |
| Day 3 | your first anomaly, and it is the only one. |
| Day 5+ | two anomalies at once |

Starting small matters. The first anomaly should arrive when you already know the room
well enough to be *annoyed* at being interrupted.

`?day=3` on the end of the address opens straight onto the first day with an anomaly in
it, so you do not have to play two quiet days every time you want to test the scary part.

## Lunchtime, beds and the mop

**Nobody is hungry all morning.** Just under halfway through the day the kitchen
opens, everybody stops what they were doing and **lines up**, and each one names
the single thing it wants. Serve them. Lunch ends when everyone has eaten, or
when lunch is simply over — a child you never fed does not keep the day in
limbo.

**Then the floor is covered in wrappers.** Take the **mop** from the bucket and
click each mess. More than a couple of them left lying about and the children
notice and get miserable, so it is not optional tidying.

**Naps happen in beds.** A sleepy monster does not want a blanket handed to it —
you **pick it up**, carry it to the nap corner, and choose **"Place in bed"**.
There are four beds and usually more than four children, which is the point.
While it sleeps it has **💤** over its head; when it has had enough it asks for
its **dummy**, and you fetch one from the nap corner to wake it up.

Because blankets are gone, **a parent whose child is missing is offered a sock**.

**The list down the side is now how you find anybody.** The building is much
bigger than the screen, so tapping a child's name opens its menu wherever it is,
and the teacher walks there.

**On a tablet** there is a thumbstick in the bottom corner. There is no action
button, because every verb comes from tapping something and picking from a menu.

## The register, the shop, and wandering off

**The register is a register**, the kind a teacher does. A desk just inside the
front door with the day's list on it: every child who arrives needs ticking off,
and the list down the side marks the ones you have missed with 📋. Ticking a
child in is worth a few coins. Leave somebody off it all day and their parent
says so at home time, however good a day the child had.

**The shop has its own room**, bottom left, past the staff room. That is where
the coins go: comfy shoes, a sock drawer by the anomaly room, a stronger door, a
bigger teapot, nicer toys — and a replacement coffee machine when you need one.

**They wander.** A child with nothing to ask for toddles off through the
doorways into any room it likes, and it keeps toddling for a few seconds after
it starts wanting something before it settles down to stare at you. **The staff
room is not for monsters.** One left in there long enough **breaks the coffee
machine**, and until you buy a new one there is no tea — which is how you keep
up with a building this size.

## Secret names

What you call your daycare is normally just a label in the corner. Two names
are not.

**The Rotted Monster Daycare.** The whole building drains to grey and black and
white, and **every single child is an anomaly**, from the first shift — no two
quiet days to learn the place. They keep their names, because the horror is
what they are, not that nobody knows who they are.

**Monster Nightcare.** The night shift, with the keys to everything: a panel of
buttons that spawn coins, children, shadow ones, toothy ones, drawings and
mess, calm everything down, make everybody happy, or skip straight to the next
shift.

Both are matched loosely, so capitals and stray spaces do not spoil it, and the
front screen whispers something as soon as the name is right.

## Wren's long game

A **shift** is a day, and the number is kept in your browser, so the story
carries on even if a run ends badly. `?shift=11` on the address jumps straight
to a beat.

### Pip, who keeps coming back

| Shift 4 | A monster called Pip visits for the first time. |
| --- | --- |
| Shift 8 | If you ever gave him a teddy, he turns up **carrying it**. |
| Shift 12 | A picture of that same teddy is **already on your wall** when the shift opens. You did not put it there. |
| Shift 20 | The front door has not opened yet, and **Pip is already here** — asleep in a bed, with the teddy, already ticked in on the register. At home time every other parent arrives. Nobody comes for Pip. |

He is never drawn from the pool of strangers: same species, same colour, same
name, every single time.

**Why he comes back** is never said out loud, because a secret you are *told*
is not a secret. It is shown: he was never collected.

### Bob's teddy

| Shift 3 | At home time a teddy is left on the playroom floor, behind the rug. It stays there, shift after shift, until somebody picks it up. |
| --- | --- |
| Shift 7 | Bob is looking for something. He does not stop and stare at you like the others do — he is not looking for you — and nothing you fetch will settle him. |
| Shift 12 | Gave it back → he is happy every shift from then on, and draws you a picture. Never found it → **his shadow turns into the horned triangle while you are watching.** He was never an ordinary child. |

### The microphone

From shift 11 a monster finds a microphone and starts announcing, over a tannoy
at the bottom of the screen. Most of it is true and pointless: *Dot just
blinked. There are three things on the floor. It is shift 11. It has been shift
11 all day.*

Then it starts announcing things that **have not happened yet** — and the game
makes them come true a few seconds later. *Wix is going to want a bouncy ball.*
*Nell is going to want something. Quite soon.* (That one is about an anomaly,
which makes the microphone a genuine early warning.) And once, if the shift is
still young: *Everyone is going home now* — and then everyone goes home.

## Playing together

One of you presses **Open a room** and reads out four letters; everybody else
picks **Join with a code**, types them in, and is dropped straight into the
same daycare. Up to five of you. There is no server of ours in the middle —
once a public broker has introduced the browsers they talk directly to each
other.

Everybody picks their own avatar: **the Teacher**, **the Robot**, or one of
three learning support assistants — **Mr Ashworth** in a cardigan, **Miss Dey**
in an apron, **Sam** in a hoodie and cap. You only ever control your own one.

**How it works.** The host runs the only simulation and is the only authority,
exactly as Wren's Kitchen does it. Guests do not simulate anything at all: they
send where they are walking and what they tapped, and draw the picture of the
building the host sends back fourteen times a second. Each guest's picture
carries its *own* menu, its own hands and its own hint line, because those are
personal. Two daycares can never disagree, because there is only ever one.

Every grown-up has their own position, hands, cup of tea, open menu and
pending walk-over-and-do-it, and a child records which pair of arms is carrying
it — so two of you can be carrying different children at once.

## How it is built

3D, in the browser, same toolkit as Wren's Kitchen: three.js with every monster built out
of plain shapes at runtime, so there are no drawings to load and a new monster is a few
lines of code.
